## Formula

    Ring k of K:  r(t) = baseₖ + A·cos(q·t + k·Δχ),   t ∈ [0, 2π]
    point (r·cos t, r·sin t);  q an integer, so every ring closes exactly
    baseₖ spans the band from inner to outer radius; Δχ is the twist per ring

## What it means

Guilloché is the engraving you have been carrying in your pocket all your life: the fine machine-turned linework on bank notes, passports, watch dials and the backs of pocket watches, cut since the 18th century on a rose engine — a lathe whose rosette cams push the cutting tool in and out as the workpiece turns. Each pass of the tool is one ring: a circle whose radius breathes sinusoidally, q lobes per revolution. Because q is a whole number, the ring closes on itself perfectly — the machine returns to its starting point tooth for tooth.

One ring is a modest cog shape. The craft — and the pattern — is in the stack. Cut K rings from inner to outer radius and advance the lobe phase by a fixed twist Δχ between passes: adjacent rings' crests and troughs interleave, and the eye stops seeing separate rings at all. It sees a woven metallic braid, diamond lattices, flowing channels — structures that exist nowhere in the ink, only in the interference between phase-shifted cosines. It is a cousin of the moiré effect, but engineered rather than accidental, which is why forgers hated it: reproducing the weave by hand requires reproducing the phase relationships exactly.

Everything here is bounded and closed by construction — sums of sinusoids never escape, never diverge, never need normalising. The mechanical pole of this collection, to the harmonograph's damped, organic pole.

## Parameters

**Rings** stacks the passes, **lobes** sets q, the teeth per revolution. **Depth** is the cut's amplitude — deep cuts overlap neighbouring rings into dense weave. **Inner** opens the central well, and **twist** is the phase advance per ring: 0 aligns lobes into radial ridges, the middle values weave, and 1 wraps back to aligned.
