## Formula

    isometric projection (cell size c — identical to voxel):
      W = (c·√3) / 2
      screenX(i,j,k) = cx + (i − k)·W
      screenY(i,j,k) = cy + ((i + k)/2 − j)·c

    unit origins, with  A = (1,−1,0),  B = (0,1,−1),  a = m+n,  b = m−n:
      δ(m,n) = (m + n) mod stagger
      O(m,n) = m·A + n·B + δ·(1,1,1)

      proj(O) = (cx + a·W,  cy + 1.5·b·c)        ← δ does not appear,
      because  proj(1,1,1) = (0, 0)

    painter's-algorithm key (box centre, drawn in ascending order):
      depth = (i₀ + sᵢ/2) + (j₀ + s_j/2) + (k₀ + s_k/2)

    disjointness clamp on arm length L, beam section w:
      L ≤ (stagger ≥ 3 ? 2 : 1) − w
      chevron with even stagger:  L ≤ (3 − w) / 2

## What it means

The motif is a small assembly of square-section beams meeting at a shared corner cube: a tripod (three arms leaving the corner along +i, +j, +k), an elbow (two perpendicular arms, with the axis triple cyclically rotated by *a* mod 3 so successive columns herringbone), or a chevron (two collinear arms split by the corner collar — a butt joint, not a mitre, because collinear beams meet at no angle for a mitre to bisect). One copy of that motif is placed at every point of the triangular lattice generated by A and B, and the whole field is projected isometrically. There is no randomness anywhere: the picture is a pure function of the parameters, which is why the pattern declares `usesSeed: false` and the file imports no PRNG at all.

The load-bearing idea is the `δ·(1,1,1)` term in the origin. The companion voxel explainer establishes that (1,1,1) is the *unique* lattice direction the isometric projection collapses to zero screen displacement — that is exactly why `i+j+k` is the only correct painter's-algorithm depth key there. This pattern takes the same fact and uses it for the opposite purpose. Shifting a unit by δ·(1,1,1) changes its depth, and therefore which of its neighbours' arms it passes in front of and which it passes behind, while moving it precisely zero pixels on screen. The tiling stays perfectly registered — every unit lands on the same lattice point it would have without the stagger — and only the over/under interleaving changes. One fact, two uses: voxel needs the direction that projects to nothing so it can *sort* by it, and isoweave needs it so it can *move along* it invisibly.

Which colouring you stagger by is not free either, and the obvious one fails. δ = (m − n) mod stagger is invariant along A + B = (1,0,−1), which is the pure horizontal screen step — so every horizontal neighbour pair would share a depth class no matter how large `stagger` grew. δ = (m + n) instead shifts the class by +1 along A, +1 along B and +2 along A+B, which separates all three nearest-neighbour steps at once. And three is a hard floor rather than a preference: the unit centres form a triangular lattice, whose chromatic number is 3, so with only two levels *some* nearest-neighbour pair always collides. That is why `stagger` 1 and 2 both render the flat, coplanar interlock and only 3 and 4 weave.

There is a second, less obvious thing `stagger` does, and it is the one you actually see. A tripod or elbow occupies an interval of width L + w on each axis, so two units are disjoint as soon as their integer offset exceeds L + w on some axis. When the nearest same-depth-class neighbour is one lattice unit away — the case at stagger 1 and 2 — that pins L + w ≤ 1, and at L + w = 1 nothing overlaps in screen space at all: arms merely butt against their neighbours'. The stagger then reduces to a permutation of the paint order over polygons that never overlap, and produces an identical *set* of polygons in a different sequence. Lifting the closest same-class offset to span 2 is what buys L + w ≤ 2 and lets an arm reach a full lattice unit past a crossing. So `stagger` is doing two jobs at once, and the visible weave is the second one. (Chevron needs one bound of its own, since its two collinear arms span 2L + w along the beam axis; at even staggers there are offsets that leave the other two axes at zero, which is where the (3 − w)/2 cap comes from.)

Because units at adjacent δ genuinely do share screen area here, the centre-depth sort has to be exact rather than merely plausible. Two boxes project onto overlapping regions exactly when the line ℝ·(1,1,1) meets the interior of their Minkowski difference, and the sign of that intersection says which is really in front; the pattern's geometry test walks every box pair across the parameter space and confirms the centre-depth key always agrees with it, with no ties among pairs that overlap. Voxel can treat `i+j+k` as an approximation; here it is a checked claim.

The last piece worth naming is what happens in `outline` and `hatch` mode. Every face there is filled with the *paper* colour and outlined in ink. That fill is not decoration — it **is** the hidden-line-removal algorithm. The faces are already emitted back to front, so a nearer box simply paints its opaque paper over the lines of whatever sits behind it, and no line clipping, no visibility test and no depth buffer appears anywhere in the code. It is the classic pre-z-buffer trick, and it is the only reason line-art mode costs the same as solid mode.

## Parameters

- **cell** — the lattice pitch in user units, and so the drawn size of a beam's cross-section and the density of units in the frame. Larger cells mean fewer units; a `MAX_UNITS` cap of 900 stops a pathological unit count at very small cells.
- **unit** — which motif is tiled: tripod, elbow or chevron. This is the actual solid being replicated, not a style, and it feeds back into the arm-length clamp (chevron carries an extra bound of its own).
- **armLength** — L, how far each arm reaches from the corner cube, before clamping. Genuinely geometric: it is what decides whether arms cross their neighbours at all.
- **beamWidth** — w, the square section of every beam and the edge of the corner cube. Also genuinely geometric, and it enters the disjointness clamp directly: a thicker beam buys a shorter maximum arm.
- **stagger** — the period of the depth colouring δ = (m+n) mod stagger. The real mathematical control: it changes the 3D arrangement without moving anything on screen, and it is what raises the arm-length clamp from 1 − w to 2 − w. Values 1 and 2 give the flat coplanar interlock, 3 and 4 give a true over/under weave with different interleavings.
- **render** — solid faces, paper-filled outlines, or hatched faces. Nominally a drawing choice, but see above: the paper fill in the two line-art modes is the hidden-line removal, not an aesthetic.
- **hatchDensity** — how many hatch lines cross a face in hatch mode. Purely a drawing knob, and ignored entirely in the other two modes; the lines are generated in the face's own (a, b) parameter space so they land exactly inside the parallelogram with no clipping.
- **faceShading** — how much darker the two side faces are drawn relative to the top face. A drawing choice standing in for a fixed light direction; it sets fill opacity in solid mode and hatch spacing in hatch mode (darker faces hatch denser), and has no effect at all in outline mode.
- **strokeWidth** — the line weight. A drawing choice, but not a symmetric one: in solid mode the stroke is a paper-coloured hairline separating faces, so 0 is a legitimate "no seams" setting, while in outline and hatch mode the ink stroke *is* the drawing, so it is floored at 0.2 to keep the page from coming out blank.
